How this works

Most permanent jobs with the City of New York run through a civil service exam. You apply during a set window, you take the exam, you get a score, and the score places you on a list for that title. What happens next depends a great deal on which job you are after, and the difference is worth knowing before you start.

Two ways this actually plays out

For many entry-level and uniformed titles, the list is the hiring queue. Police Officer, Sanitation Worker, Emergency Medical Specialist. You pass, you are ranked, and agencies work down the list as they hire. Your number is close to the whole story, and waiting is most of the process.

For many professional and managerial titles, the list is a door rather than a queue. Being reachable on the list for a title like Administrative Staff Analyst is what makes you eligible for the postings that require it. You still apply, still interview, still compete on your experience. The exam is not the thing that gets you the job, it is the thing that lets you be considered for a whole class of jobs you otherwise cannot hold permanently.

Both are the same system seen from different places in it. Which one you are in depends on the title, so it is worth asking people already doing the job rather than assuming.

The short version

Find an exam for a title you want. Apply during its application period, which is usually about a month long and is the only time you can apply. Take the exam. Get a score. What follows is either a wait to be called or a set of doors that open, depending on the title.

Step by step

1. Find an exam
Exams are announced on a schedule the City publishes once a year, and most application periods open on the first Wednesday of a month. The front page shows what is open and what is coming. If nothing is open for the job you want, that is normal. Some titles run an exam every year, some every four or five years, some almost never.
2. Read the Notice of Examination
The Notice is the real document. It lists the requirements, the fee, what the test covers, and the dates. Nothing on this site replaces it. Where a Notice has been published we link straight to the City's PDF.
3. Apply through OASys
nyc.gov/examsforjobs is the City's application system. You need an account there. There is usually a fee, and it can be waived if you receive public assistance. Apply before the last day rather than on it. The system gets busy and the deadline does not move.
4. Take the exam
Most are multiple choice at a test center. Some are physical, some are judged on your education and experience rather than a test sitting.
5. Get your score and your list number
Passing puts you on a civil service list, ranked by score. Your list number is your place in that queue. Ties are usually broken by when you filed. Veterans and some other groups can add points to a passing score, which changes where they land.
6. Be reachable, then be certified
When an agency fills a vacancy it asks for names from the top of the list, which is called a certification, and interviews from the names it gets. Being certified is not the same as being hired, and for many titles the interview still decides it. Lists usually run four years and can be extended. How much your number matters varies: for some titles it is nearly the whole decision, for others being reachable is the point and the hiring is a normal competitive process from there.

Words the City uses

Open competitive
An exam anyone can take. You do not need to work for the City.
Promotion
An exam only current City employees in a related title can take.
Title
The job itself, as the civil service defines it, with a code, a salary range and a union. Every title has a page here.
Application period
The window when applications are accepted. Miss it and you wait for the next exam.
Civil service list
Everyone who passed a given exam, in score order.
Certification
An agency pulling names off a list to fill a vacancy.
Provisional
Someone doing a civil service job without having come off a list, usually because no list exists yet. It is meant to be temporary.

Two honest warnings

It is slow. Months between applying and sitting the exam, months between the exam and the list, and possibly years on the list. People who get City jobs this way generally applied and then got on with their lives.

Dates move. DCAS extends application periods fairly often, and the open data feed this site reads can lag the City's own pages by weeks. We reconcile against those pages daily and let them win, but before you rely on a date, check nyc.gov.
